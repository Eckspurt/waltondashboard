Facilitator Briefing · Classroom Escape Room

The Locked-Out Analyst

A four-stage cybersecurity escape room blending a fake-OS laptop with physical evidence. Built around real attack patterns — OSINT, phishing, USB drop attacks, and encoding — so the puzzles double as the lesson.

Audience

Students

Group size

1–4

Run time

20–30 min

Stages

4

The Briefing

Print this as a mission card and hand it to each team at the start — it sets the premise and, deliberately, models the idea that security testing requires authorization.

Veridian Dynamics · Security Operations — Internal Memo — Authorized Access Only

Analyst Mia Torres resigned without notice last Friday. Her workstation was left logged out, and her manager flagged unusual login activity on her account earlier that week.

Before IT wipes the machine, your team is authorized to review her workstation, recover any evidence of what happened, and confirm whether her account was compromised.

You have this laptop, whatever she left on and around her desk, and each other. IT reclaims the machine at the end of the period — work fast.

Learning Objectives

  • 01
    OSINT & password guessingHow public info — résumés, LinkedIn, socials — leaks the raw material for password guesses, and why format policies don't fix that.
  • 02
    Phishing & QR-code ("quishing") attacksSpotting look-alike sender domains and unsolicited QR codes before they get scanned.
  • 03
    USB drop attacksWhy a found drive gets reported, never plugged in — and how attackers count on curiosity.
  • 04
    Encoding vs. encryptionBase64 looks unreadable but isn't protected — anyone with a decoder gets the plaintext back.

Stage 1 — The Login Screen

STAGE 01 / 04

The Login Screen

OSINT & Passwords

The monitor is already on. Someone left the login screen up — username filled in, cursor blinking in the password field.

Physical

  • Mia's printed résumé (1 page), face-up near the keyboard
  • An index card: "Veridian Dynamics Password Policy" excerpt, propped beside the monitor

Digital

  • Fake-OS login page. Username mtorres is pre-filled and locked
  • Wrong password → red shake + "Access Denied." Unlimited attempts — don't gate progress on lucky typing
Résumé excerpt
MIA TORRES — Security Analyst

PROJECTS
  Project Sparrow — led migration of internal auth service (2019–2020)
  Certified: CompTIA Security+ (2021)

EDUCATION
  B.S. Computer Science, Comment State University — Class of 2019

INTERESTS
  Trail running, cast-iron cooking, her rescue dog Biscuit

Password policy card
"All Veridian Dynamics credentials follow: [Capitalized keyword][4-digit year][1 special character]. Example: Falcon2021!"
Reveal solution
Sparrow2019!

The only bolded proper noun on the résumé is the keyword. Its own line ties it to 2019, which the Education line independently confirms — so groups don't have to guess between it and the 2021 certification (a deliberate decoy). The special character comes straight from the policy card's example.

Teaching moment: nothing here was "hacked" — it was all public. That's the point: résumés and social profiles routinely leak exactly the words and numbers people build passwords from.

Stage 2 — The Inbox

STAGE 02 / 04

The Inbox

Phishing / QR

The desktop has two icons: Inbox, and a folder called Personal. The folder is empty — a deliberate dead end.

Physical

  • A printed QR code card taped under the keyboard, mirroring the one in the phishing email

Digital

  • Fake email client with 3 messages: a spoofed "IT" phishing email with a QR code, a legitimate internal IT alert, and a decoy calendar notice
Inbox — message 1 (phishing)
From: IT-Support@veridiandynamics-verify.com
Subject: Your VPN certificate expires today — scan to re-verify

Inbox — message 2 (legitimate)
From: it-security@veridiandynamics.com
Subject: Unregistered device flagged on your workstation
"We logged an unregistered USB device on port USB-3 at 11:07 AM, Thu 10/07. Please confirm with Security if this was you."

The physical QR code, when scanned, reveals plain text (no hosting required): "Nice catch. Real Veridian Dynamics IT never asks you to scan for VPN renewal. — Security Awareness Team." It's a reward for caution, not a path forward — the room's real clue lives in the legitimate email, not the phish.

Reveal solution
1107

Port, time, and date in the legitimate IT email give the four digits needed for Stage 3's lock: 11:07 → 1107. Groups who scan the phishing QR aren't punished, but they also don't get ahead — it only confirms they correctly avoided the trap.

Teaching moment: compare veridiandynamics-verify.com against the real veridiandynamics.com domain, and note the urgency language ("expires today") — both are classic phishing tells.

Stage 3 — The Found Drive

STAGE 03 / 04

The Found Drive

USB Drop Attack

Near the desk sits a small clear case labeled "Q3 PAYROLL — CONFIDENTIAL," holding a USB drive behind a 3-digit combination padlock. A card taped beside it reads: "Found a device? Don't plug it in. Report it, then open only when authorized."

Physical

  • Dummy USB drive, sealed in a small tin or clear case
  • 3-digit combination padlock on the case
  • "Security Awareness" card modeling the correct behavior

Digital

  • None by design. Cover any spare USB port on the laptop with a printed "PORT DISABLED — SEE SECURITY POLICY" label
  • The only path forward is physical: open the case
Inside the case — folded card
V0hJU1RMRUJMT1dFUg==
"Enter the decoded phrase at the terminal."
Reveal solution
Lock: 1107 → decodes to: WHISTLEBLOWER

The lock combination is Stage 2's time (1107). Inside, the Base64 string decodes with any online decoder to WHISTLEBLOWER — the passphrase for Stage 4.

Teaching moment: the disabled port isn't set dressing — it's forcing the "don't plug in unknown media" lesson to be the only way through, instead of just a poster nobody acts on. In the debrief, name the pattern: BadUSB / USB drop attacks rely on curiosity, not technical exploits.

Stage 4 — The Terminal

STAGE 04 / 04

The Terminal

Resolution

Back at the laptop, a second desktop icon has appeared: "SecOps Terminal." Opening it shows a single prompt.

SecOps terminal
> ENTER RECOVERY PHRASE: _

Physical

  • Final lockbox with a 4-digit combination, holding prizes or completion certificates

Digital

  • Accepts WHISTLEBLOWER (build case-insensitive)
  • Success triggers a full-screen "ACCESS GRANTED" reveal and displays the final lockbox code
Reveal solution / story payoff

The reveal recontextualizes everything: Mia wasn't compromised. She's the one who caught and reported the phishing campaign targeting Finance, and resigned to join the company's new red team full-time. Her note leaves the final lockbox code (set it to match whatever combo lock you buy — e.g. 4271) and a thank-you to the team.

Teaching moment: this is the synthesis beat — the "incident" the room built toward turns out to be someone doing security right. Good hook into the debrief questions below.

Hint Ladder

Progressive hints for groups that stall. Suggested cadence: offer Level 1 unprompted around the 8-minute mark of a stage, Level 2 around 14 minutes, Level 3 only if asked directly.

Stage 1 — Login

LevelHint
1Somewhere on the desk is a document from the missing employee. Read it twice.
2The policy card tells you the exact shape of the password. Match it to something bolded on the résumé.
3Combine the highlighted project name with the year tied to that same line, then add the punctuation from the policy's example.

Stage 2 — Inbox

LevelHint
1Not every email is trustworthy. Check who actually sent each one.
2The email asking you to scan something isn't from the real IT domain — compare it letter by letter.
3The legitimate email mentions a port, a time, and a date. You'll need those numbers later.

Stage 3 — Found drive

LevelHint
1Somewhere nearby is something you were told never to plug in.
2You don't need to plug it in to get inside it.
3The combination is the time from the legitimate email, written as four digits.

Stage 4 — Terminal

LevelHint
1There's a second program on the desktop you haven't opened yet.
2Whatever was inside the case wasn't the answer — it was instructions for finding the answer.
3That jumble of letters and numbers unscrambles with a basic decoder — try any online Base64 tool.

Facilitator Ops

Reset checklist (between groups)

  • Reload the fake-OS page to clear login/terminal state
  • Re-fold the Base64 card and re-lock the case
  • Scramble the 3-digit padlock away from 1107
  • Reset the final lockbox combination
  • Re-hide the QR card, restock the lockbox prize

Watch for / correct in debrief

  • A student may reflexively plug in the dummy drive — let it fail quietly (disabled-port label), don't scold, save it for debrief
  • "Base64 looks encrypted" is a common misread — name it explicitly as encoding, not encryption
  • Post a visible timer; 20–30 min total fits most class periods with 5–10 min left for debrief

Debrief Questions

  1. What single detail made the phishing email convincing? What one detail gave it away?
  2. What's on Mia's résumé that you'd now think twice about posting publicly — and why?
  3. What should you actually do if you find a USB drive in a parking lot?
  4. Why doesn't Base64 count as "encryption," even though it looks unreadable at a glance?
  5. Who in the story had authorization to do what they did — and who granted it?
